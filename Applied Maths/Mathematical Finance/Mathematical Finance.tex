\section{Basics of Market Models}
\subsection{Modeling the Market with Stochastic Processes}
A bond is a financial asset in which you lend $£B_0$ to a bank today and received $£B_T$ at time $T\in\R_{\geq 0}$. The value of the bond at time $t\in[0,T]$ is given by $B_t$. The value of the bond is fixed and has no uncertainty. We model this as a stochastic process in the following way. 

\begin{defn}{Bonds}{}\\
Let $(\Omega,\mF,P)$ be a filtered probability space with filtration $\{\mF_t\;|\;t\geq 0\}$. A bond is an adapted stochastic process $B:\Omega\times[0,\infty)\to\R$ be an adapted stochastic process that is a finite variation process. 
\end{defn}

Recall that an adapted stochastic process is a finite variation process if the following are true. 
\begin{itemize}
\item $B_0=0$ almost surely. 
\item $t\mapsto B_t$ is a finite variation function almost surely. 
\end{itemize}

Recall that a consequence is that $B$ has no local martingale component: It has no local irreducible randomness. 

A stock is a financial asset in which you buy a share in a company at price $S_0$ today, but the price of the stock in the future $S_t\in\R_{\geq 0}$ are unknown. It depends on earning reports, competitor action, macroeconomic news and other random factors. We define more generally the concept of a risky asset. 

\begin{defn}{Risky Assets}{}\\
Let $(\Omega,\mF,P)$ be a probability space with filtration $\{\mF_t\;|\;t\in[0,\infty)\}$. A risky asset on the space is a semimartingale $S:\Omega\times[0,\infty)\to\R$. 
\end{defn}

We call them risky because the local martingale component makes the random variable truely random. 

\begin{defn}{Market Models}{}\\
Let $(\Omega,\mF,P)$ be a probability space with filtration $\{\mF_t\;|\;t\in[0,\infty)\}$. A market model on the space consists of a bond $B:\Omega\times[0,\infty)\to\R$ and $d$ risky assets $S_i:\Omega\times[0,\infty)\to\R$ for $1\leq i\leq d$. 
\end{defn}

It is important to incorporate bonds when we are modelling stock prices. This is because it is related to the time value of money. Suppose that $B_t$ is the bond at time $t$ if you lend the bank $£1$ at time $t=0$. This means the bank has deemed that the value of $£1$ today is worth the same as $£B_t$ at time $t$ in the future. This is precisely the depreciation of money. Indeed when we try to model stock prices, we need to take into account the time depreciation of money, and bonds are precisely the way to represent it. 

\begin{defn}{Discounted Values}{}\\
Let $(\Omega,\mF,P)$ be a probability space with filtration $\{\mF_t\;|\;t\in[0,\infty)\}$. Let $(B,S_1,\dots,S_d)$ be a market model. Define the discounted value of $S_i$ to be $$\widetilde{S}_i=\frac{S_i}{B}$$
\end{defn}

This is the value of the stock when the time value of money is accounted for. 

\begin{defn}{Trading Strategies}{}\\
Let $(\Omega,\mF,P)$ be a probability space with filtration $\{\mF_t\;|\;t\in[0,\infty)\}$. Let $(B,S_1,\dots,S_d)$ be a market model. A trading strategy is a predictable process $\varphi:\Omega\times[0,\infty)\to\R^{d+1}$. 
\end{defn}

Trading strategies are predictable processes because we would like the trader to only use information strictly from the past. 

\begin{defn}{Portfolio Value}{}\\
Let $(\Omega,\mF,P)$ be a probability space with filtration $\{\mF_t\;|\;t\in[0,\infty)\}$. Let $(B,S_1,\dots,S_d)$ be a market model. Let $\varphi:\Omega\times[0,\infty)\to\R$ be a trading strategy. 
\begin{itemize}
\item Define the portfolio value of $\varphi$ to be the stochastic process $V(\varphi):\Omega\times[0,\infty)\to\R$ given by $$V=\varphi_0B+\sum_{i=1}^d\varphi_iS_i$$
\item Define the discounted portfolio value of $\varphi$ to be the stochastic process $\widetilde{V}:\Omega\times[0,\infty)\to\R$ given by $$\widetilde{V}(\varphi)=\frac{V}{B}=\varphi_0+\sum_{i=1}^d\varphi_i\widetilde{S}_i$$
\end{itemize}
\end{defn}

\begin{defn}{Admissible Trading Strategies}{}\\
Let $(\Omega,\mF,P)$ be a probability space with filtration $\{\mF_t\;|\;t\in[0,\infty)\}$. Let $(B,S_1,\dots,S_d)$ be a market model. Let $\varphi:\Omega\times[0,\infty)\to\R^{d+1}$ be a trading strategy. We say that $\varphi$ is admissible if the following are true. 
\begin{itemize}
\item $\varphi$ is $\widetilde{S}$-integrable. 
\item There exists $a\in[0,\infty)$ such that $\widetilde{V}(\varphi)\geq-a$ almost surely for all $t\in[0,\infty)$. 
\end{itemize}
\end{defn}

\begin{defn}{Self-Funding Trading Strategies}{}\\
Let $(\Omega,\mF,P)$ be a probability space with filtration $\{\mF_t\;|\;t\in[0,\infty)\}$. Let $(B,S_1,\dots,S_d)$ be a market model. Let $\varphi:\Omega\times[0,\infty)\to\R^{d+1}$ be a trading strategy. We say that $\varphi$ is self financing if $$\widetilde{V}(\varphi)(-,t)=\widetilde{V}_0(\varphi)(-,t)+\int_0^t(\varphi_1(-,s),\dots,\varphi_d(-,s))d\widetilde{S}(-,s)$$ almost surely. 
\end{defn}

\subsection{Arbitrages}
An arbitrage opportunity is a trading strategy that 
\begin{itemize}
\item Requires no money upfront (zero initial cost)
\item Never leads to a loss
\item Makes a profit with positive probability. 
\end{itemize}

\begin{defn}{Arbitrage}{}\\
Let $(\Omega,\mF,P)$ be a probability space with filtration $\{\mF_t\;|\;t\in[0,T]\}$. Let $(B,S_1,\dots,S_d)$ be a market model. Let $\varphi:\Omega\times[0,T]\to\R^{d+1}$ be an admissible and self-funding trading strategy. We say that $\varphi$ is an arbitrage if the following are true. 
\begin{itemize}
\item $\widetilde{V}(\varphi)(-,0)=0$ almost surely. 
\item $\widetilde{V}(\varphi)(-,T)\geq 0$ almost surely. 
\item $P(\widetilde{V}(\varphi)(-,T)>0)>0$. 
\end{itemize}
\end{defn}

We assume that arbitrage opportunities do not exist in financial markets. 

\begin{defn}{No Free Lunch with Vanishing Risk}{}\\
Let $(\Omega,\mF,P)$ be a probability space with filtration $\{\mF_t\;|\;t\in[0,T]\}$. Let $(B,S_1,\dots,S_d)$ be a market model. We say that the market satisfies No Free Lunch with Vanishing Risk (NFLVR) if there are no sequence $(\varphi^n)$ of admissible strategies such that 
\begin{itemize}
\item $\widetilde{V}(\varphi^n)(-,T)\geq-\frac{1}{n}$. 
\item $\widetilde{V}(\varphi^n)(-,T)$ converges to $X$ almost surely where $X$ is a random variable such that $P(X>0)>0$. 
\end{itemize}
\end{defn}

\begin{lmm}{}{}\\
Let $(\Omega,\mF,P)$ be a probability space with filtration $\{\mF_t\;|\;t\in[0,T]\}$. Let $(B,S_1,\dots,S_d)$ be a market model. If the market satisfies NFLVR, then are no arbitrages in the market. 
\end{lmm}

\subsection{Financial Derivatives}
\begin{defn}{Payoffs}{}\\
Let $(\Omega,\mF,P)$ be a probability space with filtration $\{\mF_t\;|\;t\in[0,\infty)\}$. Let $(B,S)$ be a market model. Let $T\in\R$. Define a payoff of the stock with maturity time $T$ to be an $\mF_T$-measurable function $H:\Omega\to\R$. 
\end{defn}

An option is a contract that gives the buyer the right, but not the obligation to buy or sell a stock at a pre-agreed price. \\

\begin{defn}{European Options}{}\\
Let $(\Omega,\mF,P)$ be a probability space with filtration $\{\mF_t\;|\;t\in[0,\infty)\}$. Let $(B,S)$ be a market model. 
\begin{itemize}
\item A European call option is a payoff $C:\Omega\to\R$ of the form $$C=\max\{S_T-K,0\}$$ with maturity time $T$ and $K\in\R$ called strike price. 
\item A European put option is a payoff $P:\Omega\to\R$ of the form $$P=\max\{K-S_T,0\}$$ with maturity time $T$ and $K\in\R$ called strike price. 
\end{itemize}
\end{defn}

A European call option on a stock $S$ with strike price $K\in\R_{\geq 0}$ and maturity $T\in\R_{\geq 0}$ is a contract that pays its holder $$\max\{S_T-K,0\}$$ at time $T$. It gives the holder the right to buy the stock at price $K$. \\

A European put option on a stock $S$ with strike price $K\in\R_{\geq 0}$ and maturity $T\in\R_{\geq 0}$ is a contract that pays its holder $$\max\{K-S_T,0\}$$ at time $T$. It gives the holder the right to sell the stock at price $K$. \\

\begin{thm}{Put-Call Parity}{}\\
Let $(\Omega,\mF,P)$ be a probability space with filtration $\{\mF_t\;|\;t\in[0,\infty)\}$. Let $(B,S)$ be a market model with no arbitrage. Suppose that $B_t=e^{rt}$. Let $C,P$ be a European call and put option of the stock with maturity time $T$ and strike price $K$ respectively. Then we have $$C-P=S_0-Ke^{-rT}$$
\end{thm}

\section{Simplifying the Market Model}
\subsection{The Binomial Model}
The one step binomial is a simple model for stock prices and it has two possible futures: the stock either goes up or down in value. The bond grows in a fixed factor. 

\begin{defn}{The One Step Binomial}{}\\
The one step binomial is the following market model. 
\begin{itemize}
\item The probability space $\Omega=\{w_u,w_d\}$, $\mF=\mP(\Omega)$ and $P(\{w_u\})=p$ and $P(\{w_d\})=1-p$ for some $p\in(0,1)$. 
\item The filtration $\mF_0=\{\emptyset,\Omega\}$ and $\mF_1=P(\Omega)$. 
\item The bond $B:\Omega\times\{0,1\}\to\R$ given by $B_0=1$ and $B_1=1+r$ for some $r>0$. 
\item The stock $S:\Omega\times\{0,1\}\to\R$ given by $S_0>0$ and $S_1=uS_01_{w_u}+dS_01_{w_d}$ where $u>d>0$. 
\end{itemize}
\end{defn}

Notice that $S_0$ is $\mF_1$-measurable but not $\mF_0$-measurable: its value at time $1$ is not know at time $0$, while the bond is $\mF_0$-measurable because it is deterministic. 

\begin{lmm}{Arbitrage in the One Step Binomial Model}{}\\
Let $(\Omega,\mF,P,B,S)$ be the one step binomial model given by the above. Let $\varphi=(\varphi^0,\varphi^1)$ be a trading strategy. Then $\varphi$ is an arbitrage if and only if the following are true. 
\begin{itemize}
\item $V_0(\varphi)=0$. 
\item $V_1(\varphi)\geq 0$. 
\item $V_1(\varphi)(x)>0$ for some $x\in\Omega$. 
\end{itemize}
\end{lmm}

\begin{lmm}{}{}\\
Let $(\Omega,\mF,P,B,S)$ be the one step binomial model given by the above. Then it has no arbitrage if and only if $d<B_1<u$. 
\end{lmm}

\begin{prp}{}{}\\
Let $(\Omega,\mF,P,B,S)$ be the one step binomial model given by the above. Let $H:\Omega\to\R$ be a payoff. If the model has no arbitrage, then there exists a trading strategy $\varphi:\Omega\times\{0,1\}\to\R$ such that $$\varphi_0(1+r)+\varphi_1uS_0=H(w_u)\;\;\;\text{ and }\;\;\;\varphi_0(1+r)+\varphi_1dS_0=H(w_d)$$ In this case, we have $$V_0(\varphi)=\frac{qH(w_u)+(1-q)H(w_d)}{1+r}$$ where $q=\frac{(1+r)-d}{u-d}$. 
\end{prp}

This trading strategy is risk-free. 

\begin{defn}{Hedge Ratio}{}\\
Let $(\Omega,\mF,P,B,S)$ be the one step binomial model given by the above. Let $H:\Omega\to\R$ be a payoff. Define the hedge ratio to be $$\Delta=\frac{H(w_u)-H(w_d)}{(u-d)S_0}$$
\end{defn}

$\Delta$ is the number of shares you need to hold per bond.

\section{Basic Concepts in Finance}
\subsection{The Time Value of Money}
\begin{cpt}{The Time Value of Money}{}\\
The time value of money is a core financial principle that states the following. A dollar today is worth more than a dollar in the future. 
\end{cpt}

Most investments are based on this financial principle. There are a few reasons why the time value of money is relevant to the discussion of investment. 
\begin{itemize}
\item Money can earn interest over time. 
\item Risk and inflation may reduce future value. 
\item Investors prefer liquidity. 
\end{itemize}

There are three main types of financial instruments. 

\begin{cpt}{Financial Instruments}{}\\
A financial instrument is a contract that gives rise to a financial asset of one entity and a financial liability or equity instrument of another entity (IAS 32, 39). Some common types of instruments include
\begin{itemize}
\item Debt (Fixed Income): A type of investment under which the borrower or issuer is obliged to make payments of a fixed amount on a fixed schedule (e.g. bonds / loans). 
\item Equity: Equity refers to ownership in an asset or a business (e.g. stocks / shares). 
\item Derivatives: A derivative is a contract between a buyer and a seller (e.g. options / futures). 
\end{itemize}
\end{cpt}

\begin{cpt}{The Time Value of Money in Fixed Income Instruments}{}\\
Let $T\in\N$ be a unit of time (in years). Let $FV_t$ be the future value of an investment at time $t\in[0,T]$. Let $PV$ be the present value of the investment. Assuming the time value of money, let $r$ be the discount rate per period. Then the relationship between the $FV_t$ and $PV$ is described in three different equations depending the type of debt. 
\begin{itemize}
\item Discount: The investor pays an initial price ($PV$) and receives a single principal cash flow at maturity ($FV$). The relation between $FV$ and $PV$ is given by $$FV_t=PV(1+r)^t$$
\item Periodic Interest: The investor pays an initial price ($PV$) and receives cash flow at fixed time intervals ($PMT_{t_i}$ at time $t_i\in[0,T]$ for $1\leq i\leq n$), as well as a principal cash flow at maturity ($FV$). The relation between $FV$ and $PV$ is given by $$PV=\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{PMT_i}{(1+r)^i}+\frac{FV_n}{(1+r)^n}$$ Perpetual bonds give periodic interest indefinitely with no maturity date. If $PMT_{t_i}=PMT_{t_{i+1}}$ for all $i$, then we take $n$ to infinity to get $$PV=\frac{PMT}{r}$$
\item Level Payments: The investor pays an initial price ($PV$) and receives periodic payments at fixed time intervals consisting of principal cash flow and interest. At the end of each period, the annuity cash flow $(A_i)$ is given by the formula $$A_i=\frac{rPV}{1-(1+r)^{-t_i}}$$ for time periods $0<t_1<\cdots<t_n=T$. 
\end{itemize}
\end{cpt}

\begin{cpt}{The Time Value of Money in Equity}{}\\
Let $T\in\N$ be a unit of time (in years). Let $FV_t$ be the future value of an investment at time $t\in[0,T]$. Let $PV_t$ be the present value of the investment at time $t\in[0,T]$. Assuming the time value of money, let $r$ be the discount rate per period. Then the relationship between $FV_t$ and $PV$ is described in three different equations depending the type of equity. 
\begin{itemize}
\item Constant Dividends: The investor pays an initial price ($PV=PV_0$) and receives a fixed periodic dividend ($D$). The relation between $PV$ and $D$ is given by $$PV=\frac{D}{r}$$
\item Constant Dividend Growth Rate: The investor pays an initial price ($PV=PV_0$) and receives a periodic dividend ($D_{t_i}$ at time $t_i$ for $1\leq i\leq n$) with a growth factor of $g$ (so $D_{t_{i+1}}=D_{t_i}(1+g)$). The relation between $PV_t$ and $D_t$ is given by $$PV_t=\frac{D_{t_i}(1+g)}{r-g}=\frac{D_{t_{i+1}}}{r-g}$$
\item Changing Dividend Growth Rate: 
\end{itemize}
\end{cpt}

If the future value and present value are known, we may solve for $r$. In this case $r$ is called the implied return instead and it measures how much investors expect to earn from an investment. 

\begin{cpt}{Cash Flow Additivity Principle}{}\\
The cash flow additivity principle is a core financial principle that states the following. The value of a set of cash flow at a given point in time is equal to the sum of values of individual cash flow. 
\end{cpt}

\subsection{Return Measures}
To assess the performance of an investment, we use a collection of measures that are useful in different situations. They help investors understand how much they have earned relative to the amount invested. \\

\begin{cpt}{}{}\\
The rate of return $R$ of an investment is the sum $$R=\text{Risk Free Interest Rate}+\text{Premium}$$ The risk free interest rate reflects the time preferences of individuals for current versus future real consumption. There are four types of premium depending on the type of cash flow. 
\begin{itemize}
\item Inflation Premium
\item Default Risk Premium
\item Liquidity Premium
\item Maturity Premium
\end{itemize}
\end{cpt}

\begin{cpt}{Normalizing Returns}{}\\
Return measures are often per annum. Given a rate of return that is not per annum, we can normalize it to obtain a rate of return that is per annum for comparison. If $r$ denotes the quoted rate and is compounded $m$ times per year, then $$R_\text{p.a.}=(1+r)^m-1$$ If $r$ is quoted per annum but compounded $m$ times, then $$R_\text{p.a.}=\left(1+\frac{r}{m}\right)^m-1$$
\end{cpt}

\begin{cpt}{The Different Types of Return Measures}{}\\
There are different ways to measure the return of an investment depending on the scenario. Let $T$ be a unit of time (in years). For $t\in[0,T]$, denote $P_t$ the value of the investment at time $t$. Denote $C_t$ the cash flow towards the investment at end of time $t$ (e.g. $P_0=-C_0$ indicating that money is spent by the investor to put towards the investment). 
\begin{itemize}
\item Absolute Return: The naive percentage change that does not account for any other factors $$R=\frac{P_T-P_0}{P_0}$$
\item Holding Period Return: Some financial instruments (such as stocks) provide income just for holding the instrument in the portfolio. $$R=\frac{P_T-P_0+I}{P_0}$$ where $I$ denotes the total income. If the investment reports are not annual, we may take the mean (AM, GM, HM) of the rates over a year for ease of comparison. 
\item The Internal Rate of Return (Money Weighted Rate of Return): Factors in the timing of cash flows towards the investment. The internal rate of return is the percentage return required for the investment to break even with its investment value (usually used for comparing investments) given some prediction of future cash flows. It is given by solving the equation $$0=\sum_{t=0}^n\frac{C_t}{(1+R)^t}$$
\item Time Weighted Rate of Return: Does the opposite of IRR since it exclude the factor of cash flow (usually to evaluate the performance of the investment solely). Suppose that cash flow occurs at discrete times $C_0,\dots,C_{t_n}$ where $t_n=T$. We obtain $n+1$ sub-periods $[0,t_1],\dots,[t_{n-1},t_n=T]$ in which each sub-period, no cash flow occurs. Let $r_i$ denote the rate of return at the sub-period $[t_{i-1},t_i]$. If $T=1$, the time weighted rate of return is given by $$R=\left(\prod_{i=1}^n(1+r_i)\right)-1$$ If $T>1$, denote the time weighted rate of return at the $j$th year by $R_j$. Then the overall time weighted rate of return is $$R=\left(R_1\cdots R_T\right)^{1/T}$$
\item Continuously Compounded Return: As $m$ tends to infinity, $\left(1+\frac{R}{m}\right)^m$ tends to $e^R$. In this case $P_T=P_0e^R$. Motivated by this, we define the continuously compounded return to be $$R=\ln(P_T/P_0)$$ 
\end{itemize}
\end{cpt}
